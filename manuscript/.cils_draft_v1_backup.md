# A Tail-Risk Floor for Slope-and-Bias Recalibration of Drifting Gas-Sensor Arrays

> Working draft for Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems.
> Replaces the withdrawn *Measurement* submission MEAS-D-26-16493, retained at
> `manuscript/measurement_blind_draft.md` as the record of the rejected version.
> Numbers in Sections 4–5 are generated by `scripts/generate_uci360_tables.py`
> and `scripts/generate_uci360_figures.py`; see `manuscript/uci360_tables.md`.

## Abstract

Slope-and-bias correction is the cheapest way to keep a multivariate calibration
usable after the instrument has drifted, and the standard practical question is
how many freshly referenced samples it needs. Guidance in calibration transfer is
usually expressed as an average error at a given standardization-set size, which
hides the failure that matters operationally: a small reference set does not
degrade the correction gently, it occasionally destroys it. We evaluate
slope-and-bias recalibration of a five-channel metal-oxide gas-sensor array
against co-located reference-analyser values over 389 days, using a
leakage-controlled protocol in which the held-out set of each 30-day target
window is frozen before any reference is selected. Three analytes, three model
classes, and ten target windows give 30 temporal sequences and 90 decision cells
at each of ten reference budgets from 0 to 50. At two references the median cell
is unaffected, with a normalised RMSE ratio of 1.009 against the frozen model,
yet the mean ratio is 2.005 because 8 of 90 cells exceed twice the frozen error
and three invert the calibration slope, the worst inflating error 44.5-fold. The
distribution therefore fails in its right tail rather than shifting. Cells above
twice frozen error number 8 at two references, 4 at three, and **none from four
onward**, after which error falls monotonically to a ratio of 0.729 at fifty
references. Two references is uniquely unsafe for a structural reason: a
two-parameter correction fitted on two points has no residual degrees of freedom,
reproduces both references exactly, and so cannot be contradicted by the data
that produced it. A reference-panel diagnostic computed before the decision
predicts how much error will be recovered above the floor (Spearman 0.814,
p = 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ on 121 previously unused days) but cannot protect against the
tail, because it is highest exactly where the two-point fit fails. Discarding the
source model and fitting the references alone needs eight references to become
tail-safe and fifty to become competitive, so four is the floor for output
correction specifically, not a universal minimum. We recommend a hard refusal
below four references rather than a diagnostic gate, and we report the search for
a cheaper panel-side guard that did not succeed.

**Keywords:** calibration transfer; model updating; slope and bias correction;
standardization set size; gas sensor array; instrumental drift; tail risk

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Model updating is the routine response to instrumental drift

A multivariate calibration built on one instrument, at one time, in one
environment, rarely stays valid. In gas-sensing the response of a metal-oxide
array to a fixed concentration changes with sensor aging, temperature and
humidity, operating history, and the composition of the sample matrix, so the
mapping from response to concentration degrades over the deployment period
[1,2]. Field studies of low-cost air-quality devices report that a calibration
obtained in the laboratory does not transfer to deployment, and that the drift
has to be assessed over the intended operating interval rather than assumed
away [3]. The same problem in near-infrared spectroscopy motivated the
calibration-transfer literature, where the response of a secondary instrument is
standardized to a primary one, or the model is updated, using a small set of
samples measured on both [4,5].

Two vocabularies describe one activity. Calibration transfer usually treats a
spatial problem, moving a model between instruments; drift compensation usually
treats a temporal one, moving a model between times on the same instrument. The
algebra of the cheapest remedy is identical in both. If the drifted predictions
retain their ordering and only their scale and offset have moved, a two-parameter
correction of the model output restores them, and this is the method that
calibration transfer knows as slope-and-bias or bias-and-skew correction [5,6].
It is the least invasive option available, it needs no access to the original
training data, and it is the option a working laboratory reaches for first.

### 1.2 The unresolved question is the size of the reference set

Every update method needs freshly referenced samples, and those samples are the
dominant cost. In a gas-sensing deployment each one requires a co-located
reference analyser reading; in process spectroscopy each requires a wet-chemistry
assay. The operational question is therefore not which correction is most
accurate in principle but **how few referenced samples the correction can be
trusted with**.

The calibration-transfer literature is unusually explicit that this number is
small, and unusually vague about how small. Piecewise direct standardization was
introduced with a handful of transfer samples [4]; reviews describe slope-and-bias
correction as requiring only a modest set and note that the requirement depends
on how the samples span the range [5,6]; subsequent work emphasises the selection
of representative transfer samples precisely because the count is low enough for
individual samples to matter [7]. In gas sensing, reviews of calibration update
for electronic noses identify reference-sample availability as the binding
practical constraint [1]. Yet reported guidance is typically an average
performance at one or two set sizes, or a percentage of the target data, rather
than a curve over an absolute count with the failure modes attached
[8–11].

An average is the wrong summary for a decision about a minimum. A laboratory
choosing a reference budget is not trying to optimise expected error; it is
trying to avoid a corrected instrument that reads backwards. Those are different
objectives and, as we show, they are answered by different statistics.

### 1.3 What a small reference set actually does

Our central empirical observation is that the cost of an undersized reference set
does not appear in the centre of the distribution. At two references the typical
sequence is neutral: the median ratio of corrected to frozen error is 1.009,
close to no effect at all. The mean is 2.005. The entire difference is produced
by 8 of 90 decision cells, three of which invert the calibration slope and turn
the corrected instrument into one that reports high concentrations as low.

This distinction is not cosmetic. A mean-shift penalty would justify a
cost-benefit trade: accept slightly worse calibration for a cheaper panel. A
heavy right tail does not, because the loss is not bounded by anything the
laboratory controls. The correct response to a tail is a floor, and the empirical
question becomes where the tail closes rather than where the mean turns
favourable.

We also find a structural reason why the smallest panel is the dangerous one,
which we believe has not been stated in this form. A two-parameter correction
fitted on two points passes through both exactly. Its residual is identically
zero and its residual degrees of freedom are zero, so **no diagnostic computed
from the reference panel can reveal that the correction has failed**. At three
references the panel gains one residual degree of freedom and can begin to
contradict the fitted line. Two references is the largest panel for which a
slope-and-bias correction is unfalsifiable from its own data, and that, rather
than sample-size efficiency, is what makes it unsuitable.

### 1.4 Contributions

1. A budget curve for slope-and-bias recalibration on an absolute reference axis
   with ten values from 0 to 50, reporting **mean and median together**, on a
   corpus with genuine reference-analyser values rather than nominal levels.
2. The tail statistic as the decision-relevant endpoint: the count of sequences
   exceeding twice frozen error, which falls 8 → 4 → 0 and identifies a floor at
   four references.
3. A mechanism for the failures, including the exact panel geometry that produces
   slope inversion, and the residual-degrees-of-freedom argument for why two
   references cannot be checked.
4. A pre-decision reference-panel diagnostic that predicts recovered error above
   the floor and demonstrably fails to guard the floor, together with a reported
   and unsuccessful search for a panel-side guard that would have replaced the
   hard floor.
5. A scope boundary: the floor of four applies to output correction. Target-only
   refitting needs eight references to become tail-safe and fifty to overtake
   output correction, so minimum reference counts are properties of the update
   method, not of the deployment.

This paper does not propose a new correction algorithm, a new drift-compensation
model, or a new transfer-sample selection method. It does not attribute the
observed temporal change to a specific physical drift mechanism, and it does not
construct a traceable uncertainty budget for the reference values. The
contribution is an evaluated minimum and the reason behind it.

## 2. Background and problem formulation

### 2.1 Slope-and-bias correction as the minimal update

Let a calibration $f$ be fitted on source data and let $\mathbf{x}$ denote the
sensor response in a later target period. Output correction replaces $f$ by

$$\tilde{f}(\mathbf{x}) = a + b\,f(\mathbf{x}),$$

with $a$ and $b$ estimated by least squares on a panel of $N$ target-period
samples for which reference values are available. The correction assumes the
drifted model preserves the ordering of concentrations and has moved only in
scale and offset. Nothing in the feature space is altered and the source data are
not revisited, which is why the method is standard practice when the drift is
mild and the reference budget is tight [5,6].

Two properties of this estimator drive everything that follows. First, it needs
at least two distinct reference values, and with exactly two it is determined:
for a panel of two samples with reference values $y_1 < y_2$ and drifted
predictions $\hat y_1, \hat y_2$,

$$b = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{\hat y_2 - \hat y_1}.$$

The estimate is therefore a ratio of two differences, and it is unbounded as the
denominator approaches zero and changes sign with it. Second, the fitted line
interpolates both points exactly, so the residual vector is zero regardless of
whether $b$ is sensible. The estimator is simultaneously most fragile and least
checkable at $N = 2$.

At $N \ge 3$ the least-squares solution is no longer a single pairwise ratio and
the panel acquires $N - 2$ residual degrees of freedom, so a badly conditioned
panel can in principle announce itself. We return to whether it does so in
practice in Section 4.5.

### 2.2 How many standardization samples does the literature recommend?

Calibration transfer treats the size of the standardization set as small and
sample-selection-sensitive rather than as a quantity with a published minimum.
Piecewise direct standardization was demonstrated with a small number of transfer
samples spanning the calibration range [4]; the review literature describes
slope-and-bias correction as the oldest and simplest transfer method and stresses
that its adequacy depends on how well the chosen samples cover the range
[5,6]. Work on transfer-sample selection is motivated explicitly by the
observation that when the count is this low, *which* samples are chosen alters the
result [7]. The recurring recommendation is that the samples span the
concentration range, which is the design we adopt and which, as Section 4.4
shows, is also what makes the two-sample case dangerous.

In gas sensing the corresponding literature is largely algorithmic. Component
correction and orthogonal signal correction remove drift directions from the
feature space [12,13]; classifier ensembles and dynamic ensembles adapt the
decision function over batches [8,9,14]; domain adaptation and distribution
alignment transfer between batches [15,16]; semi-supervised and online methods
exploit unlabelled target data [17,18,19]; calibrant-free approaches avoid the
reference requirement altogether [20]. These contributions target accuracy under
drift and generally report a single panel design or a fraction of target data.
The complementary question of the smallest trustworthy reference count for the
cheapest correction is not usually the object of study, and reported averages
cannot answer it.

Adaptive network-calibration work on air-quality devices is closer to our
setting, updating models over time on deployed instruments [21], and the corpus we
use originates in on-field calibration of exactly this kind [22,23].

### 2.3 Estimand, endpoints, and the tail statistic

The decision unit is a **cell**: an (analyte, target window, model class) triple.
A laboratory deploys one model for one analyte and decides on an action for it, so
the action is chosen per cell. The reporting unit for association analyses is the
temporal sequence (analyte, target window), with per-model results given
separately.

For each cell and each budget $N$ we compute normalised RMSE on the frozen
held-out set,

$$\mathrm{nRMSE} = \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}}{\max(y) - \min(y)},$$

for the frozen model and for the corrected model, and form the per-cell ratio

$$r = \frac{\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{corrected}}}{\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{frozen}}}.$$

We report the mean and median of $r$, and, as the decision-relevant endpoint, the
**count of cells with $r > 2$**. The threshold of two is a deliberately coarse
statement of unacceptability: a corrected instrument with twice the error of the
uncorrected one is not a marginal outcome, it is a failed intervention. We also
report the count of cells whose held-out calibration slope is negative, which
identifies the inversion subclass.

The held-out calibration slope is the slope of a regression of reference values on
predictions computed on the test set, where unity is ideal. It is a different
quantity from the correction coefficient $b$ of Section 2.1, and the two differ by
an order of magnitude in the failure cases; we keep them notationally distinct
throughout.

Two summaries are avoided. Ratios of the form
$(\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{frozen}} - \mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{corrected}}) /
(\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{frozen}} - \mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{oracle}})$ are unstable
when the denominator is small and are reported only as secondary quantities with
denominator diagnostics. Cells are not treated as independent replicates: they
share source fits and target windows, so dispersion is descriptive and no pooled
confidence statement is made across them.

### 2.4 A diagnostic available before the decision

A quantity that is to inform the decision must be computable without the held-out
set. Let $\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{frozen}}(\text{panel})$ be the frozen model scored
on the $N$ reference samples and $\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{source val}}$ the same
model family scored on the latest source window withheld from its own training.
Define

$$D_{\mathrm{ref}}(N) = \frac{\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{frozen}}(\text{panel}) -
\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{source val}}}{\mathrm{nRMSE}_{\text{source val}}},$$

the relative degradation of the deployed model on the reference panel compared
with its own source-period performance. Both terms are available at the moment of
the decision. This distinguishes $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ from test-set degradation
measures, which are observable only after the fact and therefore cannot drive an
operational rule — a limitation of the analysis this manuscript supersedes.

The pre-specified hypotheses were: $D_{\mathrm{ref}}(5)$ is positively associated
with the absolute benefit of correction (H1); a rule that corrects only when
$D_{\mathrm{ref}}(N) \ge \tau$ beats both always-frozen and always-correct on
held-out mean nRMSE (H2); and the reference count needed for the *action* to
stabilise is smaller than the count needed for the *fit* to plateau (H3).
Section 3.6 records the provenance of these hypotheses, which is not neutral and
is disclosed as part of the result.

## 3. Materials and methods

### 3.1 Corpus

The evidence corpus is the De Vito air-quality array, a five-channel metal-oxide
gas-sensor device co-located with certified reference analysers at an urban
roadside site over 389 days from 10 March 2004 to 4 April 2005, distributed
through the UCI Machine Learning Repository [24] and originating in on-field
calibration studies of the same device [22,23].

Three analytes are used, each with a co-located reference-analyser target: CO in
mg m⁻³, NO₂ in µg m⁻³, and NOx in ppb. Features are the five metal-oxide channels
PT08.S1(CO), PT08.S2(NMHC), PT08.S3(NOx), PT08.S4(NO₂) and PT08.S5(O₃). The
sentinel value −200 is treated as missing and complete cases are formed per
analyte over the target and the five channels. The NMHC reference channel is
excluded because 8443 of 9357 rows are sentinel. Temperature, relative humidity
and absolute humidity are excluded as features so that the temporal signal is not
absorbed by contemporaneous environmental covariates.

Two properties make this corpus suitable for a claim about reference-set size,
and they are the properties the withdrawn version of this work lacked. Target
values are reference-analyser readings rather than supplied nominal levels, and
each 30-day window contains between 40 and 478 distinct reference values per
analyte (Table 1). A reference set spanning a genuine working range is a
precondition for any statement about how many samples that range needs.

A second corpus is retained for contrast only. The Wörner one-year electronic-nose
archive [25] carries two nominal concentration levels per analyte and 6–12
held-out rows per sequence. It cannot support a statement about a concentration
range and is used solely for the comparison in Section 4.7.

### 3.2 Temporal windows and leakage control

Windows are 30 days wide, counted from the first timestamp, giving 13 windows over
the 389-day span. Windows 1–3 are the source period and each of windows 4–13 is
evaluated as a target period in turn, giving 10 target windows and, with three
analytes, **30 temporal sequences** and **90 decision cells** at each budget.

Within each target window, 30 % of rows are assigned to a held-out test set,
stratified into ten quantile bins of the reference value, **before any reference
sample is selected**. A concentration stratum containing a single row is left in
the reference pool because it cannot be represented in both the calibration and
the evaluation set. The test set is then fixed for all budgets and all strategies,
so every comparison in this paper is made against an identical held-out set.
Temporal order is validated programmatically and no random split across time is
used, following the standard cautions on cross-validation under temporal
dependence [26,27,28] and on target leakage in model selection [29].

Analysis is additionally restricted to the concentration range common to the
source period and the target window, so that no strategy is credited or penalised
for extrapolating outside the range the source model was built on.

### 3.3 Reference panels

Reference budgets are absolute counts, $N \in \{0, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 20,
50\}$, and panels are **nested**: the panel at a larger budget contains the panel
at every smaller one, so budget effects are not confounded with panel identity.

Panels are ordered to span the reference range, as calibration-transfer practice
recommends [5,7]. Concretely, the reference pool of each sequence is sorted by
quantile stratum and drawn in the order lowest, highest, second lowest, second
highest, and so on. The first two samples are therefore one row from the lowest
stratum and one from the highest. This maximises the leverage available to a
two-point fit, which is the intended behaviour, and it is also the mechanism
behind the failures reported in Section 4.4. A single panel seed is used
throughout; the withdrawn analysis reported a seed sensitivity on the contrast
corpus.

### 3.4 Models and update strategies

Three model classes are used: PLS regression with two components on standardized
features, a random forest with 200 trees, and gradient-boosted trees with 300
rounds, depth 5 and learning rate 0.05. Hyperparameters are fixed in advance and
are not tuned per window, because tuning per target window would itself consume
target-period information.

Four strategies receive the identical reference panel:

- **frozen** — the source model, unchanged. The reference panel is not used.
- **output correction** — the source model with the two-parameter correction of
  Section 2.1 fitted on the panel. This is the lightweight strategy under study.
- **target-only refit** — a new model of the same class fitted on the panel alone,
  discarding the source data.
- **full retraining** — a new model of the same class fitted on the source data
  and the panel together.

At $N = 0$ the correction is undefined and every strategy degenerates to the
frozen model, which is why the $N = 0$ row of every table reports a ratio of
exactly one. This row is retained because it fixes the reference level of the
comparison.

### 3.5 Endpoints

The primary endpoint is held-out nRMSE and the primary decision statistic is the
count of cells with ratio above two, as specified in Section 2.3. Secondary
endpoints are MAE, the held-out calibration slope, and a recovery ratio reported
with denominator diagnostics. The reported association tests are Spearman rank
correlations, chosen because the relation between $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and recovered
error is not assumed linear and because the ratio scale is heavy-tailed.

### 3.6 Pre-specification, provenance, and a disclosed confound

The protocol was frozen in writing before the benchmark was executed
(`docs/uci360_primary_protocol.md`), and no threshold, window boundary, or verdict
was altered afterwards. Two features of its provenance are disclosed because they
bear on how the results should be weighted.

**The diagnostic hypothesis is exploratory in origin.** $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the
form of the decision rule were devised by inspecting an earlier three-window
analysis of this same corpus covering its first 270 days. Target windows 4–9
overlap that span and are therefore **in-sample for hypothesis generation**;
windows 10–13, covering 5 December 2004 to 4 April 2005, are 121 days that no
earlier analysis in this project examined and constitute the confirmation set.
Results are reported separately for the two sets throughout, and the held-out set
carries the evidential weight. The hypothesis is not presented as pre-registered
from the outset of the project.

**The diagnostic and the correction share a panel.** $D_{\mathrm{ref}}(N)$ is
computed on the same $N$ samples used to fit the correction. Selecting a large $N$
therefore improves the quality of the fit for reasons unrelated to the quality of
the decision, so the choice of decision budget in H2 does not isolate the
diagnostic's value. Comparisons between the rule and the always-correct baseline
at a fixed $N$ remain valid and are the ones we interpret.

Additionally, the mechanism analysis of Section 4.5 and Table S3 is **post-hoc**.
It was constructed after the failures were observed, is not among H1–H3, and is
reported as mechanism and as a negative result rather than as a validated rule.

### 3.7 Reproducibility

The benchmark was executed twice from the same configuration and seed in
independent output directories. Row keys are identical, both runs produce
2160 × 22 tables on the pre-specified grid, and the maximum absolute numerical
difference across all numeric columns is 2.487 × 10⁻¹³, consistent with
floating-point accumulation alone. Missing-value patterns are identical in every
numeric column and all three pre-specified verdicts, both decision budgets, and
the held-out association coefficient reproduce exactly. Figures are regenerated
from the result tables by script and reproduce byte-identically on re-execution.

## 4. Results

All results below are computed over 90 decision cells per budget with no skipped
sequences. Table 1 gives the corpus and evaluation design, Table 2 the budget
curve for all four strategies, Table 3 every cell that exceeded twice frozen
error, and Table 4 the pre-specified hypothesis outcomes.

### 4.1 The mean and the median disagree at the smallest budget

Figure 1 shows held-out error against reference count, with mean and median
plotted on the same axes. The two statistics agree everywhere except at the
smallest budget, and their disagreement there is the principal finding.

At two references the median ratio of corrected to frozen error is **1.009**: the
typical sequence is neither helped nor harmed. The mean ratio at the same budget
is **2.005**. Removing the eight cells that exceed twice frozen error changes the
remaining 82 from a mean frozen error of 0.191 to a corrected 0.161, a ratio of
**0.845** — that is, once the tail is excluded, two references are already
beneficial on average.

The gap closes as the panel grows. From four references both statistics fall below
one together, 0.906 mean and 0.899 median, and absolute error then decreases
monotonically in both mean and median through to fifty references, where the mean
ratio is 0.729 and 83 of 90 cells improve. The median *ratio* is not monotone over
this range, rising slightly at six and twenty references; the monotone quantities
are absolute error and the median calibration slope, and we make no claim of
monotone ratio improvement.

An average error at a given standardization-set size is therefore an incomplete
description of what a small reference set does. At two references it reports a
doubling that the typical laboratory would not experience, while concealing the
small number that would experience far worse.

### 4.2 The tail closes at four references

Figure 2 shows every cell at every budget together with the count exceeding twice
frozen error. The bulk of the distribution lies at or below one at every budget
including two references; the failures are isolated points, not a shifted mass.

Cells above twice frozen error number **8 at two references, 4 at three, and none
at four or at any larger budget**. Slope inversion, meaning a negative held-out
calibration slope, occurs in **three cells and only at two references**. All
twelve failures are enumerated in Table 3 with their mechanism.

The failures are not uniformly distributed. The three inversions are the three
model classes of a single NO₂ window, and on the held-out windows 10–13 alone two
references are already beneficial, with a ratio of 0.758 and 25 of 36 cells
improving. The tail risk is real but concentrated, and the count of eight should be
read as an upper bound on how often such a failure arises rather than as a rate
estimate for a new deployment.

We take four references as the operational floor on the basis of the tail count,
not the mean. The mean ratio first falls below one at four as well, but that
coincidence is not the argument: a floor justified by a mean would be negotiable
against cost, and a floor justified by an unbounded tail is not.

### 4.3 Where the correction goes wrong

Figure 3 shows the held-out calibration slope by budget. At zero references, that
is for the uncorrected frozen model, the median slope is 1.175: the deployed models
overpredict the concentration range and this is the distortion the correction is
asked to remove. From two references onward the median slope approaches unity
monotonically, from 0.786 at two to 0.988 at fifty.

Three cells at two references have a negative slope, minimum −0.120, meaning the
corrected model orders concentrations backwards. No inversion occurs at any larger
budget; the minimum slope at three references is +0.354.

Dispersion behaves differently from the median and is worth stating explicitly
because the intuitive reading is wrong. The standard deviation of the slope is
0.240 at two references, which is **smaller** than the 0.331 at three, because
two-point fits are displaced downward as a group rather than scattered. Dispersion
contracts decisively only at the largest budgets, 0.126 at twenty and 0.088 at
fifty. The slope distribution therefore tightens on unity late, and a claim that
precision improves steadily with reference count would not be supported by these
data.

### 4.4 Mechanism: a two-point fit inverts when the panel is compressed

Because panels are ordered to span the range, the two-reference panel consists of
one sample from the lowest quantile stratum and one from the highest. In the NO₂
window that carries all three inversions those samples have reference values of
**27.0 and 118.0 µg m⁻³**, a true gap of +91.0.

All three frozen models place them in the wrong order (Table S2). PLS predicts
116.60 and 112.33, the random forest 117.32 and 107.66, and the gradient-boosted
model 122.64 and 110.94, giving predicted gaps of **−4.27, −9.66 and −11.70**. By
the two-point identity of Section 2.1 the correction coefficients are therefore
**−21.32, −9.42 and −7.78**, and every prediction is inverted. Held-out error
inflates 44.5-, 23.8- and 19.8-fold.

The magnitude has two causes and both matter. The sign of the denominator produces
the inversion, and the smallness of the denominator produces the size: a 91 µg m⁻³
interval compressed into four units of predicted range yields a coefficient of
twenty even before the sign is considered. A correction that appears to be a
modest two-parameter adjustment is in fact a division by a quantity the laboratory
has not inspected.

The window in question is the sparsest of the ten NO₂ windows, with 362 rows and
106 distinct reference values, the lowest in mean concentration at 66.0 µg m⁻³,
and the most strongly right-skewed at 0.60 (Table S4). Its range is not the
narrowest, so the distinguishing features are sparsity and low concentration
rather than range alone. This is the regime in which a drifted model has least
resolution to order two samples correctly.

### 4.5 Two references cannot be checked, and three can only be checked weakly

The two-parameter correction fitted on two points reproduces both exactly. The
median residual RMSE over the reference panel at two references is **0.000** and
the residual degrees of freedom are zero (Table S3). No statistic computed from
the reference panel can distinguish the three inverting cells from the rest on
goodness-of-fit grounds, because all of them fit perfectly.

We searched for a panel-side guard that would permit two references with a
safeguard instead of forbidding them, and report that it does not hold.

The sign of the predicted gap over the panel does separate the inversion subclass
perfectly: the three cells with a non-positive predicted gap are exactly the three
largest inflations, and no other cell at two references has a non-positive gap.
This check costs nothing and is available before the decision, so we recommend it
as a screen. But it does not close the floor, for two reasons. First, the
remaining **five of eight** failures at two references have *positive* predicted
gaps; their failure is compression without inversion and the sign test does not
see it. Second, at three references, where a residual degree of freedom exists, the
four surviving failures look **clean on their own panel**: rank agreement between
predicted and reference ordering is 1.000 in every case and the panel correlation
exceeds 0.98, with only a mildly elevated panel residual (median 13.4 against 4.05
for the rest). Each of those panels has two closely spaced low references and one
distant high reference, so the fit is determined by a single leveraged point and is
reproduced almost exactly.

The operational consequence is that the floor has to be a refusal rather than a
gate. Below it the correction either cannot be checked at all, at two references,
or is checked against a panel geometry that hides the failure, at three.

### 4.6 The diagnostic predicts recovery but does not confer safety

Figure 4 shows $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ against realised benefit at five references and
at two, with rule-fitting and held-out windows distinguished.

At five references the diagnostic is strongly associated with the magnitude of the
benefit on the confirmation windows: Spearman $\rho = 0.814$, $p = 1.6 \times
10^{-9}$, $n = 36$, and per model 0.832 for PLS, 0.902 for the random forest and
0.881 for gradient boosting. On the rule-fitting windows, which are in-sample for
hypothesis generation, $\rho = 0.348$, $p = 0.010$. H1 is supported on the
confirmation set (Table 4).

At two references the association is much weaker and no longer consistent across
window sets: $\rho = 0.372$, $p = 0.025$ on held-out windows, $\rho = -0.120$,
$p = 0.389$ on rule-fitting windows, and $\rho = 0.201$, $p = 0.057$ pooled. More
consequential than the weakening is the direction of the failures. The three cells
with the **highest** diagnostic values at two references are the three
catastrophic ones, with $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ between 5.15 and 5.94. Stratifying by
diagnostic quartile makes the reversal explicit: at five references the top
quartile has the best mean ratio, 0.568, whereas at two references the same top
quartile has a mean ratio of 4.43 against a median of 0.665.

A gate keyed on a high diagnostic therefore recommends correction exactly where a
two-point fit is about to fail. The diagnostic answers "how much will this
recover?" and not "is this safe to attempt?", and the two questions are not
ordered the same way by the data.

H2, the rule-utility hypothesis, is technically supported and practically null.
The threshold and decision budget selected on rule-fitting windows were
$\tau = 0.10$ and $N = 20$; applied unchanged to the confirmation windows the rule
attains a mean nRMSE of 0.117582 against 0.117631 for always-correct and 0.241234
for always-frozen. The margin over always-correct is 0.0000486 nRMSE, or 0.041 %,
and the rule corrects 35 of 36 cells. It is always-correct plus rounding. The
honest reading, which the frozen protocol anticipated and declared reportable, is
that **above the floor no gate is needed**, because almost every cell benefits.
H3 is supported: action agreement with the largest-budget diagnostic reaches 100 %
at ten references while the fit itself does not plateau until twenty, so deciding
whether to correct is cheaper than correcting well.

### 4.7 The floor belongs to the correction, not to the deployment

Table 2 compares all four strategies on identical panels. Target-only refitting,
which discards the source model, is markedly worse at small budgets: mean nRMSE
0.456 at two references against 0.296 for output correction, worst ratio 40.5, and
**35 of 90 cells** above twice frozen error against eight. Its tail does not close
until **eight** references, twice the output-correction floor, and it only
overtakes output correction at fifty, where it reaches 0.1027 against 0.1056.

The two summaries disagree for this strategy in the same way they did for output
correction, which is worth stating because it is the same trap. Target-only mean
nRMSE falls below the frozen model's at four references, 0.178 against 0.184, so a
mean-based reading would license four. Its mean *per-cell* ratio does not fall
below one until eight, which is exactly the budget at which its tail closes. The
mean nRMSE improves early because the cells that do well pull it down; the ratio
and the tail count agree with each other and disagree with it.

Full retraining behaves in the opposite way. It never exceeds twice frozen error at
any budget, because the source data dominate the fit and bound the damage a bad
panel can do, but it improves slowly, from 0.173 at two references to 0.123 at
fifty, and is the least effective of the three update strategies from five
references onward.

Retaining the source model and correcting only its output is thus what makes a
small panel usable at all. It also fixes the scope of the headline number: **four
is the floor for output correction**, and minimum reference counts are properties
of the update method rather than of the instrument or the deployment.

### 4.8 The floor is only observable on a corpus with a working range

Applying the identical protocol to the two-level contrast corpus [25] at five
references gives 12 sequences improved against 12 worsened. The same protocol on
this corpus gives 59 against 31.

The difference is a property of the reference structure, and it bounds where a
claim about reference-set size can be made at all. With two nominal concentration
levels there is no range over which a slope can be estimated: the two-parameter
correction of Section 2.1 degenerates towards an offset adjustment, because $b$ is
estimated from a single contrast that every panel reproduces identically. Reference
*count* then has almost no room to act, and an evaluation on such a corpus will
report heterogeneity across analytes and models rather than a dependence on count
— not because the dependence is absent but because the design cannot express it.

This has a methodological consequence beyond the present result. Studies that
evaluate calibration update against nominal concentration levels, which is common
where reference analysers are unavailable, are structurally unable to detect a
reference-count floor. The floor reported here required targets with 40 to 478
distinct values per window (Table 1), and we would not expect it to be recoverable
from a two-level design at any sample size.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal interpretation

Slope-and-bias recalibration of this drifting array does not degrade gracefully as
the reference set shrinks. Down to four samples it is reliably beneficial and no
sequence in a 389-day corpus exceeds twice its uncorrected error. Below four the
central tendency barely moves while a small number of sequences fail severely, and
at two samples the correction can invert the instrument's response entirely. The
quantity that changes at the floor is the tail, not the average.

This reframes the practical guidance in a way we think transfers beyond this
corpus. The question "how many reference samples does a slope-and-bias correction
need?" has usually been answered with an expected error. That answer is not wrong,
but it is answering a different question from the one a laboratory asks before
committing to a correction, which is whether the correction can go badly wrong. On
these data the two answers differ by a factor of two in the recommended minimum: a
mean-based reading would tolerate two samples as roughly neutral, and a
tail-based reading forbids it.

### 5.2 Why two samples is a special case rather than a small case

The structural argument of Sections 2.1 and 4.5 is the part of this work we expect
to generalise most directly, because it is a property of the estimator rather than
of the corpus.

A two-parameter correction on two points is exactly determined. Its coefficient is
a ratio of differences whose denominator is the drifted model's predicted gap over
the panel, a quantity that is small precisely when the model has lost resolution,
and whose sign can invert precisely when the model has degraded most. At the same
time the fit interpolates both points, so its residual carries no information. The
estimator is least stable and least checkable at the same sample size. Any method
that estimates $k$ parameters from $k$ referenced samples inherits this property,
so the argument applies to bias-only correction at one sample and to higher-order
output corrections at their own exact-determination point.

This also explains why spanning the range, which calibration transfer correctly
recommends [5,7], is not protective at the minimum. Spanning maximises the
denominator in expectation and is the right design; it does not prevent the
denominator from being small or negative in the particular window where the model
has drifted worst. Good transfer-sample design and an adequate transfer-sample
count are separate requirements, and the first does not substitute for the second.

### 5.3 Relation to the calibration-transfer and drift-compensation literature

Our result is consistent with the standing description of slope-and-bias
correction as effective with a modest reference set [5,6], and it puts a number and
a failure mode on "modest" for this class of instrument. It is also consistent with
the emphasis in transfer-sample selection work on the sensitivity of the outcome to
which samples are chosen [7]: at the counts where that sensitivity is largest, we
find it is not merely a matter of efficiency but of whether the correction inverts.

Relative to the gas-sensor drift-compensation literature, this paper is
deliberately not an algorithmic contribution. Feature-space methods such as
component correction and orthogonal signal correction [12,13], ensemble adaptation
[8,9,14], distribution alignment [15,16], and semi-supervised or online updating
[17,18,19] pursue accuracy under drift and often assume a supply of target-period
data. Calibrant-free approaches [20] avoid the reference requirement entirely and
are the appropriate choice when no references can be obtained. Our contribution is
orthogonal: for the cheapest and most widely used correction, it establishes the
count below which the correction should be refused, and the reason.

The corpus originates in on-field calibration of a co-located array [22,23] and
subsequent work on the same platform has addressed adaptive network calibration
over time [21]. That line of work supports the premise that periodic output
correction is a realistic operational practice; the present result specifies its
minimum input.

### 5.4 Recommended procedure

Every step uses only quantities available before the correction is committed.

1. **Refuse output correction below four labelled target-period references.** This
   is a hard floor justified by tail risk. It is not a cost-benefit threshold and
   should not be traded against sampling cost.
2. **Inspect the panel before fitting.** Compute the drifted model's predictions on
   the reference samples and confirm that they order the samples the way the
   reference method does, and that their spread is a substantial fraction of the
   reference spread. A non-positive or negligible predicted spread is a refusal
   condition at any count. This screen catches inversion; Section 4.5 shows it does
   not catch compression, so it supplements the floor and does not replace it.
3. **Above the floor, correct without a gate.** On these data a diagnostic-gated
   rule beat unconditional correction by 0.041 %, which is not a difference worth
   operational complexity. Use $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to *anticipate* how much will be
   recovered, not to decide whether to proceed.
4. **Budget the decision separately from the fit.** The action stabilises at about
   ten references while the fit continues to improve to about twenty, so if the
   only question is whether drift warrants attention, a smaller panel answers it.
5. **Do not substitute a target-only refit at small budgets.** It requires eight
   references to become tail-safe and fifty to outperform output correction.

### 5.5 What the tail statistic costs to adopt

Reporting a tail count rather than an average requires enough sequences for the
count to be meaningful, which is the practical reason this analysis needed 30
sequences and 90 cells rather than the eight sequences of the withdrawn version.
With eight sequences, a failure rate of 8 in 90 is as likely as not to be absent
entirely, and an evaluation that failed to observe it would have reported that two
references are neutral. We therefore regard corpus scale not as a matter of
statistical power in the conventional sense but as a precondition for observing the
endpoint at all.

## 6. Limitations

**One instrument and one site.** All results come from a single five-channel array
at one urban roadside location. The floor of four is established for this
instrument class and this deployment; the structural argument of Section 5.2
should transfer, but the specific count should be re-established elsewhere before
being relied on.

**The tail is concentrated.** Three of the eight failures at two references are
the three model classes of one NO₂ window, and that window is the sparsest and
lowest in concentration of the ten. The count of eight is an upper bound on failure
frequency, not a rate estimate, and on the confirmation windows alone two
references are already beneficial.

**One panel seed.** Reference panels are drawn once. A different draw would place
different samples in the two-reference panel and could relocate which cells fail,
although the mechanism of Section 4.4 depends on the panel spanning the range,
which every draw does by construction.

**Cells are not independent.** The 90 cells share source fits and target windows.
Dispersion statistics are descriptive and no pooled confidence interval is
reported across them.

**The diagnostic hypothesis is exploratory in origin and shares a panel with the
fit.** Both are disclosed in Section 3.6. The confirmation windows carry the
evidential weight for H1, and the choice of decision budget in H2 does not isolate
the diagnostic's contribution.

**The mechanism analysis is post-hoc.** Section 4.5 and Table S3 were constructed
after the failures were observed. They are reported as mechanism and as a negative
result on a candidate guard, not as a validated screening rule.

**Temporal change is not attributed to physical drift.** We describe a change in
the joint distribution of sensor response and reference value between windows. We
do not separate sensor aging from environmental variation, maintenance, or shifts
in the concentration distribution, and we do not present the results as a physical
drift estimate. Environmental covariates were deliberately excluded as features,
which prevents the temporal signal from being absorbed but also means the analysis
cannot apportion it.

**Predictive error is not measurement uncertainty.** nRMSE and the held-out
calibration slope are predictive performance statistics. They are not statements of
trueness or of measurement uncertainty in the metrological sense, and no
traceability claim is made beyond what the reference analysers of the source corpus
document.

**Three model classes.** PLS, random forest and gradient boosting were tested.
Other model families, in particular neural approaches with different extrapolation
behaviour, may compress a reference range differently and could shift the floor.

## 7. Conclusions

For slope-and-bias recalibration of a drifting metal-oxide gas-sensor array
against co-located reference-analyser values, the cost of an undersized reference
set appears as a heavy right tail rather than as a shift in average error. At two
references the median sequence is unaffected, with a ratio of 1.009, while 8 of 90
sequences exceed twice their uncorrected error, three invert the calibration slope,
and the worst inflates error 44.5-fold. Sequences above twice frozen error number 8
at two references, 4 at three, and none from four onward, after which error
decreases monotonically to a ratio of 0.729 at fifty. Four labelled references is
therefore an operational floor, and it is a floor set by tail risk rather than by
mean performance.

Two references is not merely a small sample but a structurally unsafe one: a
two-parameter correction fitted on two points has no residual degrees of freedom,
so it reproduces both references exactly and cannot be contradicted by the data
that produced it. A reference-panel diagnostic computed before the decision
predicts how much error the correction will recover above the floor, with a
Spearman coefficient of 0.814 on 121 days not used in any earlier analysis, but it
cannot guard the floor, because it is highest exactly where the two-point fit
fails. A search for a panel-side guard that would license two references found one
check that detects inversion and no check that detects the remaining failures, so
we recommend refusal below four references rather than a diagnostic gate. Because
target-only refitting requires eight references to become tail-safe and fifty to
become competitive, the floor is a property of output correction specifically and
minimum reference counts should be reported per update method.
